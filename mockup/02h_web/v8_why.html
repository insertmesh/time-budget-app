1. The cage feeling

A budget sounds like another thing to worry about. You already feel you have too little time, and you already feel like you're not in control of it. Adding a tool that counts it can sound like adding a cage.

That feeling is common, and it has a name in psychology: reactance. When something takes away your freedom or feels like control from outside, people push back or just disappear. It shows up with money budgets, diets, planners, and "you should just manage your time better".

So here is the deal. This page tries to explain what a time budget is, why it might be worth a try, and what we know and don't know. It won't tell you what to do with your time. The app doesn't either. You can read all of it and still say no.

2. Same 24 hours

Everybody gets 24 hours a day. You can't save them, borrow them or earn extra. They get spent either way, planned or not. That's the sentence the whole app is built on:

Time is always running, whether you plan for it or not.

You've heard "you need a budget" your whole life. Yes. You know. The part that usually gets skipped is how, and what it should feel like. Most advice comes from people for whom time was never a problem, and it shows.

3. Why time is hard to see

Money leaves a receipt. Time leaves a feeling. Two things shape that feeling, and researchers who study time perception tend to separate them:

  • While it passes. The more attention you pay to time, the slower it feels. Watching the clock stretches a minute. Being absorbed in something shrinks an hour.
  • Looking back. Afterwards, an hour feels long if it held lots of different memories, and short if it was all the same. That's why a busy holiday feels fast while it happens and long in memory, and a flat week drags and then disappears.

Your body is part of the clock too. There is research suggesting that sensing your own heartbeat helps people judge intervals of a few seconds, and that hunger and other body states change how time feels. That part of the science is still early, so take it as "interesting" and not as settled.

What this adds up to: your sense of time is real, and it's also a poor measuring tool. That isn't a flaw in you. It's how attention and memory work in everyone.

Boring meeting, good game

A boring one-hour meeting feels like three. A one-hour game of something you love feels like twenty minutes. A week where every day looks the same disappears completely, and you can't remember what you did on Tuesday.

4. Everybody guesses wrong

There is a very well-tested effect called the planning fallacy: people underestimate how long their own tasks will take. It still happens when they know that similar tasks ran late before. It's stronger for your own tasks than for other people's.

So if your plans keep falling apart, one reason is plain and boring: the guess was optimistic. Everyone's are. A plan built on a guess will be off, and the fix is better information about how long things really take.

A log gives you that information. After a few days you don't have to guess how long "quick" takes. It's on the screen.

I'll just fix this one thing

"I'll just fix this one thing." It's 15 minutes. It's now 2 hours 10, you have fixed three other things, and the first one is still broken.

Four small chores, one gone afternoon

You plan dishes, laundry, a phone call and paying the bills in one afternoon. All four are small. Together they eat the whole afternoon, and what you remember is not having done them.

5. If your brain does time differently

For some brains the gap between clock time and felt time is bigger. Here is what the research says, with the uncertain parts marked as uncertain.

ADHD

"Time blindness" is a popular label, often linked to the researcher Russell Barkley. It isn't a formal diagnosis term, and not everyone with ADHD has it. What is measured: in lab tasks, groups with ADHD tend to do worse at estimating, producing and reproducing durations. A large review pooling 55 studies found this, with the strongest link for reproducing a duration. Two things seem to be involved: attention drifts off the task, and waiting feels worse than it does for others (called delay aversion). In adults the evidence is thinner than in children.

In daily life that fits a familiar pattern: long waits feel endless, and short tasks vanish. It's also why "just be on time" is not a useful tip.

Autism

The research here is mixed. A review of 45 studies found no clear agreement on whether basic timing differs. One study of autistic adults found timing tasks comparable to non-autistic adults, while questionnaires still showed differences in how time feels to them. So the honest summary is: "autistic people can't sense time" is not supported, and "time can feel different" is plausible.

Two ideas from autistic research and community are worth knowing. Monotropism is a theory that attention narrows into a deep tunnel on few things at once, which makes immersion easy and switching hard. Autistic inertia is the reported experience of finding it hard to start, or hard to stop. A study of 24 autistic adults described both, and noted they can help and hurt. Both are descriptions that many people recognise. Neither is settled science.

Either way

You don't need a diagnosis for any of this to apply. Plenty of people lose hours without noticing, or can't start things, or can't stop them. The app doesn't ask what the reason is. It just shows where the time went.

The delivery window

A delivery window of 12:00–16:00 feels like four days. You can't start anything, because it might ring in a minute. Meanwhile the three hours you spent tuning a drum sound felt like twenty minutes.

In a minute (the shower)

You know you'll take a shower. You've known for three hours. "In a minute." It's not that you don't want to. Starting just doesn't happen, until suddenly it does.

The buzz that broke the flow

You finally get into something deep, and your phone buzzes, and the whole thing falls over. The switch cost you more than the interruption itself.

6. What tends to help

  • Skills you can learn. In a trial with 88 adults with ADHD, a 12-week group program that taught time management, organisation and planning did better than a supportive-therapy group. Time skills seem teachable, and they work best when practised.
  • Putting time outside your head. Visual timers and logs make time visible so you don't have to hold it. This is plausible and low risk, and many people say it helps. Strong trials in adults are missing, so we say "plausible", not "proven".
  • Body doubling (working next to someone) is widely reported to help. Controlled studies in adults don't exist yet.
  • Plans that expect to be wrong. Because of the planning fallacy, a rigid schedule built on optimistic guesses breaks often, and that can end in shame. A plan that can flex survives a bad day.
Sick-kid Sunday

Plan: hobby day. Reality: a sick kid. A rigid plan says you failed. A flexible plan just moves the hobby to Thursday.

7. Tracking without the cage

Research on motivation (self-determination theory) says people stay with something when it supports three needs: feeling in charge of their choices, feeling capable, and feeling connected. Self-tracking can help or hurt all three. Studies of health and fitness apps found that people liked setting their own goals and getting feedback that fit them, and disliked being judged or pushed.

What matters most seems to be how much choice you feel, more than any particular rule. Here is how the app tries to build on that, and you should check whether it works for you:

  • You set the goals. Nobody else's idea of a good day is built in.
  • It shows data and not a verdict. Gaps are data. "TBH? No idea" is an accepted answer.
  • You decide when to plan, and you can always skip it.
  • An unbalanced week is allowed. Staying up late is allowed. It just shows.
  • In version 1 your data stays in your browser. Nobody else sees it.

Caveat: the studies above are about fitness and health apps, not time budgets. We found no study of time-logging apps for ADHD or autistic adults. This part is reasoning, not proof.

8. What you would get

The first thing you get is a picture: where a week of your own time went. It's usually interesting, and that's the whole reward at the start. Some questions it can answer if you ask them:

  • How long does the "quick" thing really take?
  • Which day of the week is always short?
  • What eats the gap between two tasks?
  • What did you get done on the days you thought you got nothing done?

Patterns tend to show up on their own once the data exists. After that, the goal isn't doing more with your time. It's doing less of what you don't actually value, and making room for what you do. That part is yours to decide, and the data only makes it possible to see.

If you want to try it, you could tap for a few days, look at the picture, and close the app if it isn't for you. That's a complete way to use it.

9. What we don't know

  • Whether a time budget works for you. That's what trying is for.
  • Whether logging apps help ADHD or autistic adults in general. We found no studies.
  • The research above is summarised from search results and reviews, and some points are marked as uncertain on purpose. This is not medical advice and the app is not a treatment.
